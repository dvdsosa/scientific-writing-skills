# Verbs and voice

Source: modules 1.2, 1.3, 2.1, 2.2, 2.4, 4.5.

Verbs drive the English language. Nouns slow it down. Almost every complaint that
scientific prose is "dense" traces back to one of three habits: the passive
voice, verbs turned into nouns, and the main verb pushed far away from its
subject.

---

## 1. Active voice

**The shape.** Active is *agent → verb → recipient*: `She throws the ball.`
Passive inverts it: `The ball is thrown by her.` — and often drops the agent
entirely: `Mistakes were made.`

**How to spot a passive verb.** Two parts, always:

1. a form of *to be* — is, are, was, were, be, been, being, am (also *could be*,
   *shall be*, *should be*), plus
2. a past participle of a verb that takes an object.

*She runs* cannot be made passive; *run* takes no object there. *She runs the
company* can: *the company is run by her*.

**How to convert.** Ask: **who does what to whom?** If the answer is "we", say
*we*.

| Passive | Active |
|---|---|
| My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me. | I will always remember my first visit to Boston. |
| Increased promoter occupancy and transcriptional activation of p21 were observed. | We observed increased promoter occupancy and transcriptional activation of p21. |
| By applying a 90-degree bending magnet, the spectrum of the electron beams could be observed. | We could observe the spectrum of the electron beams by applying a 90-degree bending magnet. |
| Cigarette ads were designed to appeal especially to children. | We designed the cigarette ads to appeal especially to children. |

**Three reasons it matters.**

*Accountability.* Compare *No attempt was made to contact non-responders because
they were deemed unimportant to the analysis* with *We did not attempt to contact
non-responders because we deemed them unimportant to the analysis.* The second
version makes visible that a human made a judgement call that might be wrong.
That is honest, not embarrassing.

*Readability.* *A strong correlation was found between use of the passive voice
and other sins of writing* → *Use of the passive voice strongly correlated with
other sins of writing.*

*It exposes ambiguity.* *General dysfunction of the immune system at the
leukocyte level is suggested by both animal and human studies.* Converting it
forces the question "dysfunction in whom?" and the answer has to be supplied:
*Both human and animal studies suggest that diabetics have general immune
dysfunction at the leukocyte level.*

**And it shortens.** Going active frequently dissolves a nominalisation as a side
effect:

- Passive: *The activation of calcium channels is induced by the depletion of endoplasmic reticulum calcium stores.*
- Active, literal: *The depletion of those calcium stores induces the activation of calcium channels.*
- Active, finished: *Depleting calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum activates calcium channels.*

Note that the passive had also inverted cause and effect.

**When passive is right.** It is a tool, not a sin — use it deliberately, not by
habit:

- The actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant: *The sample was collected in 2019.*
- **Methods**, where what was done matters more than who did it, where the section
  is skimmed anyway, and where the active voice forces every sentence to start
  with *we*. Mixing passive and active in Methods is fine as long as it is not
  jarring.
- When the object deserves the emphasis: *Oral temperatures were measured* puts
  the weight on the measurement rather than the measurer.

Introduction, Results and Discussion should be active.

## 2. *We* and *I* are allowed

Someone probably told you personal pronouns are unscientific. They are wrong, and
this matters because you cannot write in the active voice without them.

- Removing yourself does not make the science more objective. You designed,
  conducted and interpreted the experiments; writing as though they materialised
  on their own is misleading, not neutral.
- Putting your name on a paper *is* taking public responsibility for its content.
  Claiming the assertions with *we* is consistent with that.
- Journal editors ask for it. *Science*'s style guide: "use active voice when
  suitable, particularly when necessary for correct syntax, e.g. *to address this
  possibility, we constructed*…"
- Watson and Crick's 1953 structure-of-DNA paper opens with the word *We*.

> After all, human agents are responsible for designing experiments, and they are
> present in the laboratory. Writing awkward phrases to avoid admitting their
> responsibility and their presence is an odd way of being objective.
> — *Science*, 1957, quoted in module 2.2

## 3. Strong verbs

Do not reach for any old verb; pick the right one. A precise verb absorbs an
adverb and often a clause.

- *reports approximately* → **estimates**
- *estimates … in the next 25 years* → **projects**
- *guards against malignant transformation* → **prevents cancer**
- *came from speakers* → **exploded from speakers**
- *the arena moved* → **the arena shook**
- *the crowd got to its feet* → **the crowd leaped to its feet**
- *scientists can take out a tumor and look at the cells* → **slice** cells out and **scoop** them from the brain

*To be* verbs — is, are, was, were, be, been, am — are unavoidable but should not
dominate. If they do, the prose has no motion.

## 4. Nominalisations — the smothered verb

The pattern: take a lively verb, turn it into a noun, then pair it with a limp
verb to carry the grammar.

| Smothered | Resurrect |
|---|---|
| provides a review of | reviews |
| offers confirmation of | confirms |
| provides a description of | describes |
| performs an analysis of | analyses |
| conducts an assessment of | assesses |
| obtains estimates of | estimates |
| makes an adjustment to | adjusts |
| makes a decision | decides |
| shows a peak | peaks |
| achieves a reduction in | reduces |
| has seen an expansion in | has expanded |
| provides a methodologic emphasis | emphasises methodology |
| results in recruitment of | recruits |

**Heuristic:** flag every `noun + of` and ask whether a direct verb exists.
Suffixes worth watching: *-tion, -sion, -ment, -ance, -ence, -ity, -ness,
-ization*.

**Why it matters beyond style.** Nominalisations let you be vague without
noticing:

> *During DNA damage, recognition of protein one by protein two results in
> recruitment of protein three and repression of cell proliferation genes.*

When Sainani tried to edit this, she could not — the nouns had concealed which
protein did what to which. She had to ask the author to draw a picture. The
rewrite is both shorter and more informative:

> *During DNA damage, protein one recruits protein two and protein three, which
> together repress cell proliferation genes.*

Nouns hide agency; verbs force you to name it.

## 5. The buried predicate

Readers wait for the verb. Until they have it, they do not know where the
sentence is going. Put too much between the subject and the main verb and you
lose them.

> *One study of 930 adults with multiple sclerosis (MS) receiving care in one of
> two managed care settings or in a fee-for-service setting found that only
> two-thirds of those needing to contact a neurologist for an MS-related problem
> in the prior 6 months had done so.*

The subject is *one study*; the verb is *found*, twenty-four words later. The fix
required no cutting at all — only moving the verb up and setting the description
aside in commas:

> *One study found that, of 930 adults with multiple sclerosis (MS) who were
> receiving care in one of two managed care settings or in a fee-for-service
> setting, only two-thirds of those needing to contact a neurologist for an
> MS-related problem in the prior six months had done so.*

Once the reader has the verb, they tolerate the description happily.

**On thresholds.** The course names no number. `prose_audit.py` flags twelve or
more words as a prompt to look, not a verdict. Some long subjects read fine; some
short ones do not. And there is a licensed exception: **a dash lets you violate
this**, because the reader finds the verb immediately after the dash (see
`punctuation.md`).

## 6. The verb check — a revision move worth its own name

When a passage feels flat and you cannot say why, do this instead of rereading it
for the fifth time:

1. Take two or three paragraphs.
2. Underline the main verb of every sentence.
3. Look at the list on its own.

Then count. How many are *to be* verbs? How many are passive? How many sit far
from their subject? Changing four or five verbs will often lift the whole
passage, and this diagnostic takes two minutes.

`prose_audit.py` reports the *to be* density and the passive count so you can do
this at scale, but doing it by hand on one paragraph teaches you more.
