# Clutter lexicon

Source: Writing in the Sciences, modules 1.4, 1.5, 2.4, 4.5.

> The secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components.
> Every word that serves no function, every long word that could be a short word,
> every adverb that carries the same meaning that's already in the verb, every
> passive construction that leaves the reader unsure of who is doing what — these
> are the thousand and one adulterants that weaken the strength of a sentence.
> — William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*, quoted in module 1.4

The tables below are lookup material. Load this file when you are actually
editing; do not try to hold it in your head.

---

## 1. Dead weight — the author clearing their throat

Delete these outright. If the point is that something is well known, a citation
at the end of the sentence says so with no words at all.

| Phrase | Do this |
|---|---|
| As it is well known | delete; add citations |
| As has been shown / demonstrated / reported | delete; add citations |
| It should be emphasized / noted / stressed that | delete |
| It can be regarded that | delete |
| It is worth noting that | delete |
| It is important to note that | delete |
| It is interesting to note that | delete |
| It is well established that | delete; add citations |
| Needless to say / It goes without saying | delete |
| In my/our opinion | delete (you are the author; that is understood) |
| To the best of our knowledge | keep only when staking a genuine novelty claim |
| As we can see from Figure 2 | "Figure 2 shows", or just cite it in parentheses |
| In this paper we will present | delete; just present it |

Worked example (module 1.4):

- Before: *As it is well known, increased athletic activity has been related to a profile of lower cardiovascular risk, lower blood pressure levels, and improved muscular and cardio-respiratory performance.*
- After: *Increased athletic activity lowers cardiovascular risk and blood pressure and improves fitness.*

Note what happened beyond the opener: *a profile of* went (blob), *levels* went
(the noun already says it), *improved muscular and cardio-respiratory
performance* became *fitness*, and *has been related to* became a direct verb
because the evidence supports the stronger claim.

## 2. Blobs — words too vague to make a picture

> Some words and phrases are blobs. — Zinsser

They feel substantial and add nothing, because the reader cannot form a concrete
image from them.

`basic tenets of` · `a profile of` · `the field of` · `in terms of` · `with
regard to` · `in the context of` · `a variety of` · `various different` · `the
process of` · `the concept of` · `the nature of` · `the presence of` · `in this
regard` · `molecular species` · `physiologic` · `methodologic` · `the occurrence
of` · `levels of` · `type of`

Test: can the reader draw it? *Molecular species* could be anything. *Physiologic
activity* is so broad it excludes nothing.

## 3. Long where short would do

| Long | Short |
|---|---|
| due to the fact that / owing to the fact that / in light of the fact that | because |
| despite the fact that / in spite of the fact that | although |
| in the event that / based on the assumption that | if |
| at the present time / at this moment in time | now |
| a majority of / the vast majority of | most |
| a number of | several |
| a large number of / a small number of | many / few |
| are of the same opinion | agree |
| less frequently occurring | rare |
| give rise to | cause |
| have an effect on | affect |
| in order to / for the purpose of / with the aim of | to |
| on the basis of | from |
| in the absence of | without |
| prior to / subsequent to | before / after |
| it is possible that | may |
| is capable of / has the ability to | can |
| all three of the | the three |
| the neonatal population | newborns |
| have long been known to be | are |
| muscular and cardio-respiratory performance | fitness |
| gliomagenesis | the formation of glioma |
| utilize | use |
| in a rapid manner | rapidly, or pick a faster verb |

These phrases never carry the main idea. Choosing the long version only slows
the reader down for the same payload.

## 4. Redundancy — the modifier already lives inside the word

| Redundant | Why |
|---|---|
| successful solutions | there is no such thing as an unsuccessful solution |
| completely eliminate | eliminate is already complete |
| future plans | plans are about the future |
| past history | history is past |
| unexpected surprise | a surprise is unexpected |
| currently underway | underway is current |
| end result / final outcome | a result is an end |
| advance planning | planning happens in advance |
| new innovation | an innovation is new |
| absolutely essential | essential is absolute |
| first of its kind **and** proof of principle | the same claim twice |

### The thesaurus trap

When you catch yourself reaching for a synonym to avoid repeating a word, ask
first whether you need the second instance at all. Usually you do not.

- Before: *…studies that illustrate the methodologic challenges or that demonstrate successful solutions to the difficulties inherent in biological research.*
- After: *…examples that illustrate challenges and solutions.*

*Illustrate* and *demonstrate* were the same word; *challenges* and
*difficulties* were the same word. One *illustrate* carries over both objects.

The other half of the repetition problem: **whole clauses that restate each
other**.

- Before: *A robust cell-mediated immune response is necessary. A deficiency in this response predisposes an individual towards active TB.*
- After: *Deficiency in T-cell-mediated immune response predisposes an individual to active TB.*

Saying a thing is necessary and saying bad things happen without it are the same
statement.

## 5. Negatives → positives (module 1.5)

Almost every negative has a positive form, and the positive is clearer. Double
negatives make the reader do algebra.

| Negative | Positive |
|---|---|
| she was not often right | she was usually wrong |
| she did not want to perform the experiment incorrectly | she wanted to perform the experiment correctly |
| they did not believe the drug was harmful | they believed the drug was safe |
| not honest | dishonest |
| not harmful | safe |
| not important | unimportant |
| does not have | lacks |
| did not remember | forgot |
| did not pay attention to | ignored |
| did not succeed | failed |
| not sufficient | insufficient |
| did not differ | was similar / unchanged |

## 6. Superfluous *there is / there are* (module 1.5)

The expletive construction pushes the real subject out of the subject position
and forces a "to be" verb. Removing it usually surfaces a better verb.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| There are many ways in which we can arrange the pulleys. | We can arrange the pulleys in many ways. |
| There was a long line of bacteria on the plate. | Bacteria lined the plate. |
| There are many physicists who like to write. | Many physicists like to write. |
| The data confirm that there is an association between vegetables and cancer. | The data confirm an association between vegetables and cancer. |

Note the second one: *was* became *lined*. That is the real prize.

## 7. Needless prepositions

Small, but this is where you find the last twenty words when an abstract is over
its limit.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The meeting happened on Monday. | The meeting happened Monday. |
| They agreed that it was true. | They agreed it was true. |
| the occurrence of Alzheimer's disease | Alzheimer's disease |

## 8. Adverbs

Adverbs feel natural in speech and email; in writing they almost never help. You
do not make a statement more powerful by adding *very* — you make it weaker,
because the reader now knows you needed a booster.

Cut on sight: `very` `really` `quite` `basically` `generally` `actually`
`essentially` `extremely` `highly` `greatly` `considerably` `substantially`
`clearly` `obviously` `certainly` `definitely` `relatively` `somewhat` `rather`
`fairly` `simply` `merely` `totally` `completely` `entirely`

Reserve `significantly` for its statistical meaning only.

For other adverbs, ask whether a better verb absorbs the adverb:

- *reports approximately* → **estimates**
- *estimates something about the future* → **projects**
- *guards against malignant transformation* → **prevents cancer**

## 9. The undo key

Cutting your own words is hard. You wrestled the sentence onto the page and it
now sounds right from sheer repetition. The practical answer: take the word out,
read the sentence without it, and remember you can always put it back.

> I've said, "Well, I really like that word *very* in there. I don't want to
> take it out." But I convinced myself to try the sentence without it, knowing
> that I could always control-Z the word back in. And I've never put the word
> back in. It's always better without it. — module 1.4

One more, to show what the reward looks like:

- Before: *Brain injury incidence shows two peak periods in almost all reports: rates are the highest in young people and the elderly.*
- After: *Brain injury incidence peaks in the young and the elderly.*

The first sentence is not wrong. The second has power.
