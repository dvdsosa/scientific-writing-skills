# Paragraphs and the upshot method

Source: modules 3.4, 3.5, 3.6, 4.1.

The paragraph is the unit of composition. Think in paragraphs when you organise a
manuscript, and use paragraph breaks to tell the reader you are switching ideas.

---

## The rules

**One idea per paragraph.** Scientists routinely stuff several into one and end
up with a block that meanders. When a paragraph changes subject, split it — do
not paper over the seam with a transition word. (A real example: an author wrote
about prior studies, then switched to possible mechanisms, and inserted *however*
to cover the join. *However* signals contrast; this was a change of topic. The
fix was two paragraphs.)

**Keep them short.** Two to five sentences is what professional writing looks
like. Short paragraphs are more focused, and they leave white space on the page.
A reader who sees an unbroken block already knows it will be a slog.

**Give away the punchline early.** Scientists think *details → supporting data →
conclusion*. Invert it when you write: conclusion first, then support. Journalism
calls this the inverted pyramid. Making the reader wade through details before
they know the point is expensive for them and cheap for you to fix.

This is not the same as mandatory topic sentences. Being forced to open every
paragraph with a formal statement of its aim is monotonous. Know your point and
signal it early; do not turn it into a ritual.

**Let logic carry the flow, not signposts.** Order events in time, earliest
first. Move from general to specific. If the underlying argument is sound, the
reader follows without being told where you are going.

**Two transition words.** *But*, to signal a change of gear. *And*, to add. That
is nearly all professional writing uses. *Nevertheless*, *on the other hand*,
*furthermore* are not more sophisticated — they are heavier, and a paragraph
where most sentences open with one is a paragraph whose logic is not doing its
job. Readers get whiplash from four *howevers*.

**First and last sentences are what gets remembered.** Make them the good ones.
Build toward the last.

## A well-built paragraph

From Obama's *Wired* essay (module 3.4). Worth studying because you can see every
device working:

> *This kind of progress hasn't happened on its own. It happened because people
> organized and voted for better prospects; because leaders enacted smart,
> forward-looking policies; because people's perspectives opened up, and with
> them, societies did too. **But this progress also happened because we scienced
> the heck out of our challenges.** Science is how we were able to combat acid
> rain and the AIDS epidemic. Technology is what allowed us to communicate across
> oceans and empathize with one another when a wall came down in Berlin or a TV
> personality came out. Without Norman Borlaug's wheat, we could not feed the
> world's hungry. Without Grace Hopper's code, we might still be analyzing data
> with pencil and paper.*

- One idea: science has been integral to human progress.
- Exactly one transition word in the whole paragraph — *but*, at the pivot.
- General → specific: general reasons for progress, then the one reason that
  matters here, then concrete instances of it.
- Two parallel pairs (*Science is how… / Technology is what…*, *Without
  Borlaug's wheat… / Without Hopper's code…*) doing the work transitions would
  otherwise do.
- Semicolons holding a list whose items carry internal commas.

## The upshot method

This is the repeatable procedure for revising a paragraph that meanders. It is
the single most useful technique in the course, and it works because it separates
*deciding what to say* from *deciding how to say it* — trying to do both at once
is what produces bloated paragraphs in the first place.

**Step 1 — Reduce each sentence to its upshot.** One short, plain line per
sentence. Do not try to make it sound good; do not preserve detail. Just the
point.

**Step 2 — List the upshots together.** Repetition that was invisible across 200
words of prose becomes obvious in eight short lines.

**Step 3 — Delete duplicates and reorder.** Group like with like. Put the main
point first.

**Step 4 — Write the paragraph from the reordered list**, at sentence level, with
the clutter and verb rules applied.

**Step 5 — Check back against the list.** Every point present, nothing added.

### Worked example (module 3.6)

Original, 139 words, on headache classification. The upshots:

1. Headaches are important.
2. Headaches have many causes.
3. The IHS classifies headaches by cause.
4. The IHS classifies headaches into primary and secondary.
5. Secondary headaches arise from another condition, such as brain tumours.
6. Primary headache disorders are disorders themselves.
7. Examples of primary: tension-type and migraine.
8. Other classification systems also exist.

Seen as a list: 3 and 4 are the same statement, so they collapse into one.
Secondary was defined before primary, which is backwards — the natural order is
primary first, and each definition should sit next to its own examples.

Result, 76 words:

> *Headache is a pain symptom that almost everyone experiences. The International
> Headache Society groups headaches into two types based on cause: primary
> headache disorders and secondary headache disorders. In primary headache
> disorders, the headache itself is the main complaint; the two most common types
> are episodic tension-type and migraine. Secondary headache disorders result
> from an underlying condition, such as a brain tumor, a systemic infection or a
> head injury.*

### A second example, and what it teaches (module 3.6)

A paragraph on chocolate and diabetes, 129 words. Three separate sentences all
said "it is surprising and counterintuitive that chocolate might be healthy." In
prose that was invisible; as three adjacent lines in a list it was unmissable.
Two were cut and the survivor moved to the end, where it lands better.

The other lesson from that edit: the author's name and the journal name for each
cited study were dropped. In this paragraph they were detail that competed with
the main idea. **Which details survive is part of the edit**, not something to
preserve by default.

### When you can see the logic, write it out

For a limitations paragraph (module 3.5), Sainani wrote an explicit outline
before editing:

```
Main idea: address criticisms about the choice of perfume concentration
  A. High concentrations can be uniformly aversive
     → we standardised intensity to a reference, so this should not apply
  B. Appropriate concentrations should produce wide variation in preference
     → we saw wide variation, with two exceptions
```

212 words became 91, and the outline made it checkable. She does not normally
write these down — but the thinking happens either way, and writing it down is
what you do when a paragraph resists.

## Sometimes the answer is not prose

While editing a taxonomy paragraph, her first instinct was that it should have
been a table — three levels of nested terms, which prose renders as a maze and a
small table renders instantly. Same for a participant flow, an experimental
setup, or a causal model.

Before polishing a paragraph, ask once whether it wants to be a table or a
diagram. See `sciwrite-manuscript/references/tables-figures.md`.
