# Punctuation and sentence variety

Source: modules 3.1, 3.3.

Cutting clutter does not mean writing only short, simple sentences. Prose made of
nothing but short declaratives is monotonous. To vary sentence structure you need
more than the comma and the period, and the four marks below are the tools. You
may have been told they are too informal for scientific writing. They are not —
pick up any professional writing and you will find them everywhere, and they
appear in *Nature Physics* papers too.

## Power to separate

Strunk and White's framing, which is the useful way to hold these in mind:

```
comma  <  colon  <  dash  <  parentheses  <  semicolon  <  period
weakest                                                   strongest
```

The dash and parentheses are slightly *less formal* than the others. That is not
a prohibition; it means use them sparingly so they keep their force.

---

## Semicolon

**Use 1 — join two independent clauses** whose connection matters.

> *But he knew something about the world; he also cared about it.*

A comma would lose the emphasis on the second clause. A period would sever the
link between the two ideas. The semicolon keeps both.

> *It was the best of times; it was the worst of times.*

**Use 2 — separate list items that themselves contain commas.** Once items have
internal punctuation, commas can no longer mark the boundaries.

> *It happened because people organized and voted for better prospects; because
> leaders enacted smart, forward-looking policies; because people's perspectives
> opened up, and with them, societies did too.*

---

## Parentheses

For an afterthought, an explanation, or a non-essential detail. The test: the
sentence must be grammatically complete and unchanged in meaning without the
material inside.

Putting something in parentheses **gives the reader permission to skip it**. That
is the feature, and it is also the warning: never park essential information
there.

> *They also have a specialized tail, kind of like a monkey's tail, that allows
> them to cling to a piece of grass (or a lucky diver's finger).*

A whole sentence may go inside parentheses. This is allowed, and it is where a
joke or an aside can live without disturbing the argument.

---

## Colon

Always follows a complete clause — whatever precedes it needs a subject and a
verb. It introduces: a list, a quote, an explanation, a conclusion, or an
amplification. It has more effect than a comma, less power to separate than a
semicolon, and more formality than a dash.

**Setting up a reason, with emphasis:**

> *That's one reason why I'm so optimistic about the future: the constant churn
> of scientific progress.*

**Building to a punchline:**

> *The woman suffers from lack of experience and a chronic democratic disease:
> compound sentences.*

**Collapsing three flat sentences into one:**

> Before: *Many types of cells in tissue develop a kind of directionality.
> Certain events happen toward one end of the cell or tissue or the other. It's a
> phenomenon called cell polarity.*
>
> After: *Many cells and tissues develop a kind of directionality, called cell
> polarity: certain events happen toward one end of the cell or tissue.*

**Colon vs semicolon when both sides are complete sentences.** With a colon you
are promising that the second clause amplifies or explains the first. The
semicolon merely links. If the first clause sets up the second — "there is a
reason, and here it is" — the colon is correct and the semicolon loses the setup.

### Two ways colons go wrong

*Grammatical mismatch.* If you announce nouns, deliver nouns.

> Wrong: *Two aspects of alcohol use are related to brain injury: as a factor associated with risk of an injury, and as a factor in TBI diagnosis…*
>
> Right: *Two aspects of alcohol use are related to brain injury: its association with risk of injury and its post-injury influences on diagnosis, recovery and survival.*

*Reversed order.* The setup comes first, the list second.

> Wrong: *In one project we have a nutritionist, a psychologist, statisticians, a computer specialist, and dietitians: a whole range of specialties.*
>
> Right: *In one project we have a whole range of specialties: a nutritionist, a psychologist, statisticians, a computer specialist, and dietitians.*

## Dash

The most versatile mark you have. Use it to add emphasis, or to drop an abrupt
definition, list or description into the middle of a sentence.

> *…it shows how humans — through our ingenuity, our commitment to fact and
> reason, and ultimately our faith in each other — can science the heck out of
> just about any problem.*

**Why the dash and not a comma or parentheses.** This is the part worth
internalising, because the three are not interchangeable.

> *The drugs did more than prevent new fat accumulation. They also triggered
> overweight mice to shed significant amounts of fat — up to half their body
> weight.*

With a comma, the magnitude loses its emphasis, which is the whole point of the
sentence. With parentheses, you signal the reader may skip it — you have just
told them that losing half their body weight is optional detail.

> *Researchers who study shipworms say these mislabeled animals — they're clams,
> not worms — are actually a scientific treasure.*

Commas fail outright here: *they're clams, not worms* is a full clause, so commas
produce a run-on. Parentheses fail differently: the material is essential —
skip it and *mislabeled* means nothing.

**The dash suspends the buried-predicate rule.** A dash can carry an enormous
insertion between subject and verb and still read, because the reader finds the
verb immediately after it closes. Thomas Friedman once ran roughly eighty words
between two dashes and the sentence still works.

**The only real caution: do not overuse it.** A dash in every sentence loses its
impact and reads as informal. Reserve it for the tough jobs — when a more common
mark would be inadequate.

---

## Rule of three

When the number of examples is arbitrary, three is a pleasing number: enough to
make the point, not so many that it overwhelms. This is a default for doubt, not
a law.

## Parallelism (module 3.3)

Ideas joined by *and*, *or*, *but* — and every item in a list — must share the
same grammatical structure. Pick a structure and stay in it.

**Pairs**

> *The velocity decreased by 50 percent, but the pressure decreased by only 10 percent.* (subject–verb / subject–verb)
>
> *We aimed to increase the resolution and to improve picture quality.* (infinitive / infinitive)

**Lists**

> Broken: *Locusts denuded fields in Utah, rural Iowa was washed away by torrents, and in Arizona the cotton was shriveled by the blazing heat.*
> (subject–verb, then passive, then prepositional phrase)
>
> Fixed: *Locusts denuded fields in Utah, torrents washed away rural Iowa, and blazing heat shriveled Arizona's cotton.*

> Broken: *…you must study hard, critically think about the medical literature, and you should be a good listener.*
>
> Fixed (all verbs): *…you must study hard, listen well and think critically about the medical literature.*
> Fixed (all nouns): *…you must be a good student, a good listener and a critical thinker.*

> Broken: *four distinct phases: (1) establishing measurement instruments; (2) pattern measurement; (3) developing interventions; and (4) the dissemination of successful interventions.*
>
> Fixed: *…establishing, measuring, developing and disseminating.*

> Broken: *…five principles: speed, real-time delivery, integration into workflow, simplicity and to avoid data entry.*
>
> Fixed: *…speed, real-time delivery, integration into workflow, simplicity and the avoidance of data entry.*

Reading a list aloud is the fastest detector: the item that breaks structure is
the one that sounds wrong.

**Parallelism also works across sentences**, and there it becomes a flow device
strong enough to replace a transition word:

> *Science is how we were able to combat acid rain and the AIDS epidemic.
> Technology is what allowed us to communicate across oceans…*
>
> *Without Norman Borlaug's wheat, we could not feed the world's hungry. Without
> Grace Hopper's code, we might still be analyzing data with pencil and paper.*

And in a technical register:

> *Classic epidemiologists pose a question about the etiology of a disease in a
> population of people. Clinical epidemiologists pose a question about the
> prognosis of a disease in a population of patients.*

The matched structure carries the contrast, so *on the other hand* becomes
unnecessary and was deleted.
