# The sections, one by one

Source: modules 5.2, 5.4, 5.5, 5.7, 5.8. Further reading the course recommends:
Thomas Annesley's series in *Clinical Chemistry* (free online, not chemistry-
specific), and Mimi Zeiger, *Essentials of Writing Biomedical Research Papers*.

---

## Verb tense — one rule for the whole manuscript

**Past tense** for completed actions: the experiments, the analyses, what you
found, what other studies found.

> *We found that… · The average reaction time was… · Miller et al. found…*

**Present tense** for assertions that are still true when the reader reads them:
what a table or figure shows, what the data suggest, what you believe.

> *Figure 1 shows… · Table 1 gives… · The findings confirm… · The data suggest…*

This holds in Methods, Results and Discussion alike. Both tenses routinely appear
in one paragraph, and that is correct:

> *Information was available for 7,766 smokers. Of these, 1,216 were classified
> as hardcore smokers. **Table 1 gives** the characteristics of…*

## Results

Write this immediately after the tables and figures; it falls straight out of
them.

**The one big mistake: reading the table aloud.** Because Results follows from
the tables, there is a pull to walk the reader through every number in order.
That is not what the section is for.

> *The mean age of the bad witches was 45 ± 5, and the mean age of the good
> witches was 36 ± 6. Gender was similar between the groups with 85 % female in
> the bad witches and 83 % female in the good witches…*

The reader has the table. Your job is the higher-level summary: point out simple
relationships, describe big-picture trends, refer to the table for the detail.

> *The witches were on average lean and predominantly female (Table 1). Bad
> witches were significantly older, had higher blood pressure, exercised less,
> and were more likely to smoke than good witches. More bad witches were
> unemployed, but this difference did not reach statistical significance.*

Note also what is **not** there: a sentence saying "Table 1 shows the descriptive
characteristics of the groups." That is obvious from the table. Launch into the
finding and cite the table in parentheses.

**Complement rather than repeat.** If a bar graph has no precise numbers, give
one or two in the text. If the table gives two means, the text can give the
percentage difference — *good witches exercised twice as long as bad witches*
rather than restating 60 and 30 minutes.

**Do highlight the key numbers.** In a placebo-controlled trial of a
blood-pressure drug, the between-group difference in reduction is the number the
study exists to produce. That one belongs in the text.

**Other rules:**

- Negative results matter as much as positive ones.
- With a control group, the treatment-versus-control comparison is the one to
  foreground.
- Reserve *significant* for statistical significance.
- Do not mix in Methods. Justify the statistical approach in Methods; in Results,
  say what the models revealed. Authors very often explain their model choice
  here, and it confuses the reader.
- Do not mix in Discussion. Results is what the data show; Discussion is what
  they mean.
- Subsections with informative headings help when results are long.
- **Use the active voice.** The fear that every sentence will start with *we* is
  unfounded here: the participants, the measurements, the models and the data are
  all available as subjects.

## Methods

The recipe. Enough for someone else to replicate the study — while minimising how
hard that is to read.

**What goes in** (varies by field; this is the shape):

- Materials: reagents, chemicals, cell lines, hardware — with the specifics that
  make them reproducible (pH, temperature, doses, versions, part numbers).
- Ethics approval, if human or animal subjects. This needs an explicit statement.
- Experimental protocol.
- How each variable was measured, and with what instrument.
- The analysis.

Annesley's checklist is a good pass: **who, what, when, where, how and why**.
Who maintained the records? What was used? When was it done? Where were things
kept?

**Three ways to make it readable:**

1. **Subsections with informative headings.** Readers skim Methods hunting for
   one specific thing; headings let them find it. Real examples: *general
   approach / biosafety / isolation of the virus / serologic analysis* — or
   *subjects / experimental protocol / hardware / GPS data processing / wind*.
2. **Cite rather than repeat.** For a well-known method, or one you described in
   an earlier paper: *as previously described [12]*.
3. **Diagrams and tables.** A participant flow chart conveys instantly what a
   paragraph of numbers conveys tediously. Complex protocols and dosing schedules
   are usually better as a diagram or table. This is underused.

**Passive voice and jargon are both acceptable here** — this is the one section
where the rules relax. What was done matters more than who did it; most readers
skim; and the active voice tends to produce a wall of sentences starting with
*we*. Mixing passive and active is fine as long as it is not jarring. The active
voice is equally acceptable if you prefer it; the choice is yours.

## Introduction

Easier than people expect, because it has a definable format — and shorter:
**typically three paragraphs, never more than five.**

**The mistake:** treating it as an exhaustive review of the general topic. You
have read a hundred papers and want to use them. That is not what this section
is for.

**The introduction is shaped around your specific question.** For a study on
smoking and breast cancer you do not review breast cancer, and you do not review
smoking. You review the potential link between the two, and only studies that
addressed that link. This narrowness is what makes it easy to write.

**The cone — broad to narrow:**

1. **What's known.** Brief context on your specific topic.
2. **What's unknown.** The gaps and limitations in those previous studies. Why is
   the question still open?
3. **Your specific question, hypothesis or aim** — stated explicitly, with the
   words: *we hypothesised that*, *we asked whether*, *our aims were*, *we
   investigated*. Reviewers scan for exactly this phrasing to find out what the
   paper is about; make it easy to find.
4. **Enough of your approach** to show how your study fills those gaps — bigger,
   prospective, a better measure, a population nobody studied.

Roughly one paragraph each for known, unknown, and your study, though authors
often alternate between known and unknown in the early paragraphs.

**Summarise the literature at a high level.** Not *Jones et al. did a
case-control study with problems x, y and z, and then Smith et al. did a
cross-sectional study with problems a, b and c*. Rather: *of nine studies, two
found an association and seven did not*, and then the shared limitations,
broadly.

**Other rules:**

- Three *short* paragraphs, not three long ones.
- Write it for a general audience; save technical detail for Methods.
- No results, no implications. You are setting the question, not answering it.

A two-paragraph example that does everything (BMJ Christmas issue, academic
spam): paragraph one is entirely background; then one sentence of unknown — *few
studies have focused on academic spam*; then the study — *we investigated the
amount, relevance, content, and suppressibility of academic emails*. Note that
the four named objectives are the four things the Discussion will answer, in
order.

## Discussion

The inverted cone. The introduction ended with the question; the discussion
starts by answering it, then broadens.

This section gives you the most freedom and is therefore the hardest, and the
best place to show what you can do as a writer.

**The shape:**

1. **The answer.** First sentence: *We found that…*, answering the question posed
   at the end of the introduction. State explicitly whether the findings are
   novel.
2. **Key secondary findings**, if any.
3. **Context.** Possible mechanisms or pathways. How your results fit the
   existing literature — confirming, contrasting.
4. **Strengths and limitations.** At least one paragraph.
5. **What's next.** Unanswered questions, what needs confirming.
6. **So what.** Implication, speculation or recommendation. Why should anyone
   outside your niche care? This is where you make them care.
7. **Conclusion.** Restate the main finding and give a take-home message.
   Repeating it is deliberate — it is the emphasis.

**Do not start with the limitations.** A real discussion opened *This
meta-analysis is subject to a number of limitations.* Start with *We found*; bury
the limitations several paragraphs down.

**Do not travel too far from your data.** Speculation is fine near the end,
clearly marked. But the main conclusions must report what you actually found, not
what you hoped to find, and you should not spend paragraphs discussing something
you did not measure. Waffling because the data did not cooperate is visible from
outside.

**The limitations paragraph is where good papers separate themselves.** Most
authors write generic boilerplate that could apply to any study. What impresses a
reviewer is a limitations section that names the concerns *they* were forming as
they read:

> The mark of a good paper, for me, is when authors have anticipated my
> criticisms and concerns… Even if they don't have a good answer for those
> limitations, just the fact that they recognize them goes a long way to
> improving my confidence in the study. — module 5.7

So: read your own paper as a hostile reviewer, list what you would attack, and
address those things specifically. Better still, address them with an analysis —
one course example excluded participants on confounding medications and reported
that the results held.

Discussion length in the course's examples ranges from four to seven paragraphs.

## Abstract

Write it **last**. *Abstract* means *to pull out*: you are pulling snippets from
the finished paper, which is why it is easy once the paper exists.

**Throw away any abstract you wrote months ago for a conference.** The pull to
reuse it is strong — it is already written, why waste work? But it was drafted
before the analysis, tables and story were settled, and it will not fit the final
paper. Starting the whole manuscript from it is worse. Bin it and start over.

**What goes in:**

1. One sentence of background and motivation.
2. The research aim or question, in the same explicit phrasing as the
   introduction: *we asked whether*, *we hypothesised that*.
3. A minimal overview of the methods.
4. A few key results, a few important numbers. Not many.
5. A brief conclusion answering the question asked.
6. **One sentence of implication, speculation or recommendation.** Easy to omit
   and important to keep: it is the *why should I care*, and it is what a
   journalist or a reader outside your field scans for.

Structured abstracts (with mandated subheadings) are easier than free-form ones.
Either way, the abstract must stand alone — it is the part most people read, and
often the only part.

Word limits are where your clutter-cutting skills pay for themselves.
