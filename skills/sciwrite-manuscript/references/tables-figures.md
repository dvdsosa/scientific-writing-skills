# Tables and figures

Source: module 5.1.

Build these **first**, before any prose. The tables and figures contain the story
of the paper; the writing around them is, in a real sense, advertising for them.
Editors, reviewers and readers often look at them before reading a word — a
reviewer typically skims the abstract, then jumps straight to the data to form
their own judgement before reading the authors' interpretation.

Two consequences. First, they must **stand alone**: a reader must make sense of
them without going back to the text, so every acronym, unit and experimental
detail needed to read them lives in the title, legend or footnotes. Second, each
one must **make a clear point**, and together they must progress through the
story. If you cannot say what a table's point is, it is not ready.

Use the fewest that tell the story. Never present the same data as both a table
and a figure.

---

## Table or figure?

**Figures** for visual impact; trends and patterns; a quick story; showing all
the data points rather than summary statistics; showing distribution or range;
highlighting the single most important result.

**Tables** for precise values, and for many variables at once. A figure does not
convey a number to the third decimal.

If a figure is turning out complex, that is a signal the data belong in a table.
A figure that needs study is a failed figure.

---

## Table format

**Title** — identifies the specific point of the table, concisely, and says what
statistics to expect: *Descriptive characteristics of the two treatment groups,
mean ± SD or n (%)*. Repeat the exact keywords used in the column headings and
the main text.

**Three horizontal rules. No vertical rules.** One above the column headings, one
below them, one below the data. This is the convention in almost every journal.

The full-grid table is worth calling out specifically, because it costs more than
it looks like it should:

> I've reviewed many, many papers over the years. And at some point I noticed
> that papers with tables like this, with all the grid lines, tend to have lots
> of problems elsewhere in the paper… So when I flip to the tables and I see all
> those grid lines, there's a red flag that goes off in my head. I automatically
> expect that the paper is going to have other problems. I suspect that other
> reviewers and editors have formed the same association. — module 5.1

Alternating row shading is fine and looks professional, as long as the three
rules are still the only rules.

**Footnotes** — symbol conventions differ by journal (asterisks, superscript
letters). Do not guess: open a published paper from the target journal and copy
what they do. Use footnotes to say exactly which groups were compared and with
what test, and to define acronyms and experimental details.

### The five ways tables look unprofessional

1. **Extra grid lines.** As above.
2. **Misaligned columns.** Especially numbers with decimals. Mixed capitalisation
   between entries. Sloppiness here reads as sloppiness everywhere.
3. **Too many significant figures.** Statistical software emits BMI to three
   decimals and people paste it in. Report only as many decimals as the
   measurement supports — for clinical or physical variables, usually one.
4. **Missing units.** *Exercise: 30* — thirty minutes a day? a week? *Age: 45* —
   years, presumably, but months if these are infants. Easy to lose when pasting
   from software output.
5. **Extra columns.** An "overall" column in a two-group comparison dilutes the
   comparison and can be computed from the other columns anyway. A column of
   exact p-values, where a star and a footnote would flag significance, does the
   same. Extra columns detract from the point.

**Do not reinvent formatting.** Capitalisation, alignment, italics, footnote
symbols: pull a published paper from your target journal and copy it. Reviewers
and editors notice tables that look like the journal's own.

## Figures

### Three kinds

**Primary evidence** — gels, micrographs, photographs, x-rays, pathology slides,
oscilloscope captures. These show data quality, and they carry a seeing-is-
believing weight that no summary statistic does. Video counts too, and carries it
even more strongly.

**Graphs** — the data visualised.

**Drawings and diagrams** — underused, and powerful. Use them for an experimental
setup, a participant or data flow, a causal or cycle relationship, a hypothetical
model, or to give readers a picture of something they cannot see. Information
that is genuinely visual is tedious in prose and instant in a drawing. In one
course example, dog-bite and cat-bite locations went into a body diagram instead
of a table, and both key messages — most bites are on the hands, and cat and dog
bites differ in location — became visible immediately.

### Legends

Every figure needs one, because the figure must stand alone: a brief informative
title conveying the main point; the experimental details a reader needs; symbol,
line and panel definitions; and the statistical detail — which test, which
comparison, what the p-values were.

### Graph types

| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| Line graph | trends over time, dose, age — anything increasing or decreasing |
| Bar graph | comparing groups at one time point or dose; easy to read, quick story |
| Scatter plot | relationship between two continuous variables; shows every data point |
| Histogram | distribution of one variable |
| Box plot | distribution and spread across groups |
| Survival curve | time-to-event |

Scatter plots deserve their reputation: they show all the data, including the
variation and non-linearity that summary statistics hide. In the course's
example, a scatter plot revealed both that one group had far wider variation than
its mean suggested and that the relationship was curved rather than straight —
neither visible from a table of means.

### The trend-line warning

A superimposed line can manufacture a relationship that is not there. In the
course, the same scatter of unrelated x and y was shown twice, once with a
downward line and once with an upward line; each time the eye saw a correlation.
Remove the lines and there is none. Superimpose a fit only when the relationship
genuinely has that shape.

### Making graphs easy to read

- Label groups meaningfully instead of using arbitrary symbols. **T** and **C**
  beat filled circles and open triangles, because the reader never has to look
  at the legend.
- Use colour. The black-and-white photocopy era is over.
- Put sample sizes on the bars. Readers want *n* and almost never get it.
- Shade bars; leave nothing at low resolution.
- If stars mark significance, make it unambiguous which comparison they refer
  to. Ambiguous stars are the single most common defect in published figures.
- Do not add a bar graph summarising data a line graph already shows. Averaging
  sessions 5–10 into a bar adds nothing that a star on the line graph would not,
  and it clutters the figure.
