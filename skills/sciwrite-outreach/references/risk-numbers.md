# Presenting risk

Source: module 8.1.

People are bad at risk. We are frightened of low-probability events and
insufficiently worried about common ones — anxious about terrorist attacks,
relaxed about heart disease, which is far more likely to kill us. Presenting risk
transparently is a genuine public service, and it is a skill.

---

## Two rules

**1. Whole numbers over percentages and fractions.** *8 more cases per 10,000
women per year* lands where *0.08 %* does not.

**2. Absolute risk over relative risk.** Relative risk without a baseline is
misleading, and reliably frightening.

If an exposure raises cancer risk from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000, you can
truthfully call that a **doubling of risk**. You must also give the absolute
values, or the reader has no way to know the increase is one case in ten thousand.

---

## The case study: the Women's Health Initiative

Worth knowing in full, because it shows how much control the researchers have.

**The background.** A large randomised, double-blind trial of post-menopausal
hormones versus placebo, begun in the 1990s when it was taken for granted that
hormones protected women against heart disease — dogma that had never been tested
in a randomised trial.

**The result.** At an interim analysis in 2002 the dogma turned out to be wrong:
women on hormones had significantly increased risk of both breast cancer and heart
disease. The trial was stopped early. At that moment **14 million post-menopausal
women in the United States were taking hormones.** The findings were a bombshell,
and the researchers had to report them without terrifying 14 million people.

**The same finding, three ways.**

*Relative risk* — as published in the JAMA paper:

> Hormones versus placebo: 1.26 for breast cancer, 1.29 for heart disease.
> A 26 % increased risk of breast cancer; a 29 % increased risk of heart disease.

Dramatic. Frightening. And if the press release had led with these, the coverage
would have led with them too.

*Absolute risk* — also in the paper:

> Invasive breast cancer: 0.30 % in the placebo group versus 0.38 % in the hormone
> group. An increase of 0.08 % per year. For heart disease, 0.07 % per year.

Now it is clear that any individual woman's risk rose very little — while across
14 million women, that still means many additional cases. Both facts are true and
both matter; only the second framing lets a reader hold them together.

*Whole numbers* — as the press release put it:

> Breast cancer rose from 30 cases per 10,000 women per year to 38 — an increase
> of 8 cases per 10,000. Heart disease rose by 7 cases per 10,000 per year.

**What happened.** Every major outlet covering the story used these numbers. Most
did not report the relative risks at all.

*(Note: relative and absolute figures will not divide out to each other exactly,
because they come from different models. If you notice they do not line up, that
is why.)*

---

## The lesson

Whoever drafted that press release thought hard about the presentation of the
numbers, and as a result an enormous, alarming public health story was reported
accurately rather than sensationally.

You have the same lever. Give journalists the transparent numbers and they will
use them. Give them nothing and they will find the most dramatic figure in your
paper.
