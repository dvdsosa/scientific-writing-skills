# Social media for science communication

Source: module 8.6 (Amy Adams, Director of Science Communication, Stanford).

This is about talking to a **general audience**. Scientists also use these
platforms to talk to each other, which is a different and also valid use.

The reason to bother: that is where the people are. If you want to be heard, be in
the room with the most people in it.

---

## Decide what you want first

Social media takes real time. Before spending it, know what you are there for:

- promoting your institution and its research
- promoting a cause you care about
- establishing yourself as someone worth following on a topic
- engaging people in conversation
- driving traffic to a website or blog
- building a personal presence in a field
- finding like-minded people

Different goals point at different platforms.

---

## The platforms, and what each is good for

**Short-form microblogging (Twitter/X and successors)** — the largest reach, the
smallest bites. An engaged audience that actively follows topics and news
outlets. Good for reaching people who do not already know you.

**Facebook and Instagram** — a moderate audience, more room per post, and heavily
image- and video-driven. As an individual you are limited to people you already
know, so the realistic use is **educating the friends you already have** — people
who do not know what doing science is like.

**Blogs, and long-form platforms like Medium** — unlimited space to explain
anything, and the smallest readership. The hard part is not writing them; it is
getting anyone to read them. A blog needs another channel driving traffic to it.

---

## What works

**Hashtags put you in front of people who do not know you exist.** Someone
following `#climate` will see your post even if they have never heard of you. Do
not hashtag every word — it is annoying and wastes space.

**Handles work the same way.** Mentioning an institution or a colleague puts the
post in front of their followers.

**Images matter.** Posts with images get more engagement, more shares, more clicks
through to the link. This is not a preference; it is how the platforms work.

**A link gets you past the character limit.** If the post is compelling enough,
people click through to the full piece.

**Choose a profile image that reads at thumbnail size.** A group photo of you and
your entire lab becomes an unidentifiable blob in a feed. Pick a handle that
identifies you.

---

## The thing that actually matters: be a person

Compare three posts about the same story:

> **Institution:** *A team led by Carolyn Bertozzi has developed a new approach to
> cancer immunotherapy.* [link, image]
>
> **Institute account:** *Carolyn Bertozzi created a chemical lawn mower as a novel
> method for cancer immunotherapy* `#cancer` `#immunotherapy`
>
> **A person:** *I always enjoy working with Carolyn Bertozzi. This time she
> created a novel method for cancer immunotherapy.*

An institution posting institutionally is correct. **An individual posting
institutionally is a mistake** — you sound aloof, remote and unreal, and few
people will follow you.

If someone follows *you*, it should be because they learn something about you:
what you are doing, what you find interesting, what it is actually like to be a
scientist. That is the thing only you can supply.

---

## Engage, do not teach

The most common failure: an account that posts nothing but educational nuggets.
Ask who would follow that. Someone who wants to be taught your field is probably
already studying it.

Social media is better used to get people thinking about something they are *not*
already doing — which means conversation rather than instruction.

---

## Before you jump in

**Lurk first.** You do not need an account to read. Follow a set of scientists,
science journalists and organisations for a couple of weeks and watch what
succeeds, what tone works, and what style would be natural for you. A column-based
client (TweetDeck-style) lets you watch several hashtags and handles at once.

Note that following a hashtag is invisible — the people whose posts you read do
not know you are reading them. The corollary matters more than the mild
voyeurism: **when you post, you do not know who is watching.** It travels further
than you think.
