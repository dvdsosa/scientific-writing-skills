# Talking with journalists

Source: module 8.1.

If a journalist gets in touch after you publish, engage. It is how the work
reaches beyond the people who read the journal.

---

## What the journalist actually wants

Usually not the science. They have often understood that already, and will ask if
they have not. What they are after is what they cannot get from the paper:

- **The big-picture significance.** The take-home message in your own words.
- **How it affects people.** Even for basic laboratory work, even in the long
  term.
- **The news hook.** Why is this being written about now? What is new or different
  about your results compared with what came before?
- **Descriptive, quotable language.** Metaphors, colourful description, something
  with bite. Give them prose and they will quote it.
- **Stories and anecdotes.** How you came up with the idea. Anything surprising,
  paradoxical or ironic — if you set out to make a weight-loss drug and it turned
  out to work better against cancer, that is the story.
- **The human dimension.** For a longer feature: what motivates you, what keeps
  you up at night. Readers want to read about people.
- **A first quote.** Most stories about a single study need an opening quote
  giving the sweeping significance of the work. Have one ready.

If you are being asked to comment on someone else's work, note that bold
criticism and strong praise are the things most likely to be printed.

---

## How to prepare

**Write down two or three take-home messages in advance.** Clear, short, active
voice, no jargon. You can read them out during the interview; they are what you
will be quoted on. Without them, the journalist has nothing quotable and will
paraphrase you instead.

**Speak as if to an intelligent relative** — an uncle, a grandparent — who is not
a scientist and whom you are talking to over dinner. That register is close to
what the article needs.

**Do not be wishy-washy.** Say clearly what you think the findings mean and what
the journalist should focus on. Hedging leaves the framing to someone else.

**Anticipate the misreadings.** Say them out loud: *here is something people might
misunderstand about this work, and here is why it is wrong.* Especially for
anything contentious.

**Volunteer the limitations.** The journalist may not ask. Giving them helps put
the work in context and keeps the story from overstating it — which protects you
as much as the reader.

**Anything controversial or biting will be quoted.** That is fine, if you meant
it. Decide before you say it.

---

## Numbers

Think carefully about how you hand over numbers, because the ones you make easy
to use are the ones that get printed.

- **Whole numbers beat fractions and percentages.** Most people understand *1 in
  100* better than *1 %*.
- **Absolute risk, not relative risk.** See `risk-numbers.md` — this one matters
  enough to have its own file.
- **Anchor unfamiliar units.** A nanometre means nothing to most readers. *One
  billionth of a metre — a human hair is about 80,000 nanometres wide, and a
  sheet of paper about 100,000* gives them something to hold.

The principle behind all three: **if you do not give journalists the transparent
numbers, they will use the most dramatic ones in your paper.** You have more
control over the coverage than you think.
