Source: modules 8.4, 8.5 (the interviewing section with Amy Adams, Director of

# Part 1 — The news story

The bread and butter of science writing: most stories report a single recently
published paper. The format is as scripted as a journal article, which makes it
straightforward to master. Typical length 500–800 words.

Underneath everything, the old newsroom check: **the five Ws and the H** — who,
what, where, when, why, how.

```
lead  →  nut graf  →  first quote  →  body  →  kicker
```

## Lead

The first paragraph. **The hardest part to write** — often more time goes into the
lead than into the whole rest of the story.

It must grab attention: catchy, short, usually one or two sentences. It conveys
the heart of the matter without giving away all the Ws, and it should complement
rather than repeat the headline. Good verbs help. A surprising fact, a vivid
description, or a person's story can all carry a lead.

> *When the Neanderthals and other prehistoric human cousins went extinct around
> 30,000 years ago, they didn't disappear completely. A little part of them lives
> on in many of us.*

Flavour and enticement, not detail.

## Nut graf

*Graf* is newsroom shorthand for paragraph. The nut graf gives the nut of the
story: who, what, why, when, where, how. It tells the reader what the story is
about.

**This is what inexperienced writers leave out.** Do not keep readers guessing;
you will lose them.

> *In 2010, scientists revealed that sporadic couplings between our ancestors and
> the Neanderthals — as well as a related group, the Denisovans — left many of us
> with traces of their DNA in our genomes. But the evolutionary impact was
> unclear. Now a team of scientists led by Peter Parham, professor of structural
> biology and of microbiology and immunology, has shown that these genetic
> exchanges significantly strengthened modern human immune systems.*

## First quote

Shortly after the nut graf. Usually a big-picture statement of why the research
matters.

> *"This is really the first evidence that there was something functional that was
> contributed from this admixture that was useful for modern humans," says Laurent
> Abi-Rached, a research associate in Parham's lab and first author on the report
> in* Science.

Quotes do several jobs: they give a human dimension, provide evidence from named
experts, allow opinions the reporter cannot state as fact, add colour, and break
up the text.

**Attribution conventions.** Name before *said* or *says* — *says Parham*, not
*Parham says* — **unless** a long description follows the name, in which case put
the verb last so it does not strand at the end.

**Stick to *said* and *says*.** Other verbs carry connotations you may not intend:
*noted* implies the statement is a fact you are affirming; *remarked* implies
casualness; *cried*, *exclaimed*, *enthused* are overt.

## Body

The details: what led up to the study, the research question, the key experiments
and findings, and — if there is room — caveats and an outside commentator. Use
quotes throughout to break it up.

## Kicker

The ending. Leave the reader satisfied, with a parting thought.

Two reliable techniques: **circle back to the lead** (started with a patient? say
what happened to them; started with an image? return to it), or **end on a quote**,
so the scientist gets the last word.

> *"This is all just speculation, but we have been apart for all this time, so it
> would be very surprising if there weren't differences," Parham says. "It would
> solve a long-standing puzzle."*

Note the placement: *Parham says* sits in the middle so the paragraph ends on the
quote, not on the attribution.

# Part 2 — Interviewing a scientist

The challenge is not understanding the science. It is getting the scientist to
talk about it in a way a general reader would understand, and with some of the
excitement they actually feel.

## Why scientists will not drop the jargon

- It is how they talk all day, to everyone.
- The jargon words are *precise*, and they are worried about being
  misinterpreted — which is reasonable.
- They genuinely do not know what non-specialists know. A concept fundamental to
  their daily work is hard to see as unfamiliar.
- Some of them do not much care whether the public understands. That is why
  writers exist.

## Why you need them to anyway

You want to **quote** them. Quotes make scientists real and believable to a public
that may be sceptical or intimidated — people excited about their work, going to
the lab to learn something new. And despite the jargon, scientists have often
spent years developing analogies and descriptions that work. Get those out of
them.

**Your job in the room is to appear not to be a scientist.** Check your ego at the
door. Asking the stupid question is what gives them permission to use smaller
words.

## The four questions

1. **"Can you describe the key finding?"** — Open here, always. It makes them step
   back and think big-picture, and it tells you what *they* consider important. You
   may have read the paper and formed a view; you did not do the work. Ask this
   first or your later questions will pull the interview somewhere else.
2. **"Why is this important?"** — Context, and the frame for the story.
3. **"How does that work?"** — The detailed questions. Ask until you actually
   understand it, because you cannot write accurately about something you do not
   follow.
4. **"Is there anything you want to add?"** — Possibly the most important question
   in the interview. This is where you get the side comments, the perspectives
   that did not fit in the paper, the things you would never have thought to ask.
   Ask it before you think the interview is over, because it usually generates
   follow-ups.

Also worth asking, for the emotional register: who benefits from this work? What
did you think when you got the result — excited, confused, surprised? What made
you ask this question in the first place?

## Editing quotes

**Use a quote as it was said.** Quotation marks mean someone really said this.

The exception is politeness: tidy up filler. *Be polite, in the sense that you
should, tidy up, you know, the quote* → *be polite, tidy up the quote*.

The Poynter Institute's rules of thumb:

- Edits must be **truthful** — never change what the speaker meant.
- **Removing is safer than adding.** Cutting *um* and *you know* is fine; adding
  words can distort meaning.
- **Be careful with slang and dialect.** If that is how someone speaks, leave it.
  Never edit a quote *into* dialect, and do not standardise a regional expression
  just because you would say it differently.

## The Q&A format

Good when the scientist speaks well, when the person *is* the story, when you want
their opinion specifically, when you are short on time, or when you are walking
readers through a process.

Same editing rules. The answer as printed must reflect what the person intended,
but it is not a transcript — most people are not interesting for several
unedited paragraphs. Cut repeated ideas and tighten.

**Interview differently for a Q&A.** Write the questions in advance and give them
a story arc, so the finished piece goes somewhere. Prepare more than you need —
about seven, to publish about five — because some answers will turn out
uninteresting and you need to be able to drop them.

And **listen for completeness**: in a Q&A you cannot slip in context afterwards
the way you can in a news story. If they did not say it, it is not there.
