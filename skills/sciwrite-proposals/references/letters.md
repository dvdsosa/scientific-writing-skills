# Letters of recommendation and personal statements

Source: modules 7.5, 7.6.

---

# Part 1 — Writing a letter of recommendation

## Before agreeing

**If you cannot write a strong letter, decline.** You are doing the candidate a
favour. A lukewarm letter is worse than a missing one, and letters exist in which
the writer got the candidate's gender wrong — which should reflect on the writer
and instead sinks the candidate.

**Calibrate to the stakes.** A small internal travel grant or a teaching
assistantship where nearly everyone succeeds needs you to vouch that the person
is a student in good standing; agreeing is low-risk even if you barely know them.
A competitive fellowship or admission needs you to know them well. Turn those
down if you do not.

**Never ask a candidate to draft their own letter.** It is common practice in
parts of academia and it defeats the entire purpose, which is an *independent*
assessment. If you are only willing to edit their draft, decline instead.

The failure mode is not hypothetical: a reader once noticed the same sentences in
two letters from entirely different organisations. The application went straight
off the pile — not because the candidate was weak, but because the letters no
longer said anything about her that she had not written herself.

**If you agree, ask for:** their CV, specifics about what they are applying for
(so you address the qualities that matter *for that*), a firm deadline you put in
your calendar, and the submission link or address.

---

## Format

Make it look like a letter: date, the committee's address, letterhead. Avoid *To
whom it may concern* — address the specific person or committee where you can.

**Opening paragraph:** how you know the candidate, for how long, and a one- or
two-sentence overview of your opinion.

> *I am pleased to recommend Sally for admission to Timbuktu Medical School.*

**Body:** the qualities that matter for this specific position. Quantitative
skills if they will work with numbers. Communication, written and spoken.
Collaboration. Initiative. Prioritisation. Creativity. Citizenship — do they go
out of their way to help others?

If they are weak somewhere relevant, say so — you can use positive framing:
*is working on that*, *has made progress in that area*.

**Closing paragraph:** a final assessment and an offer to answer questions.

---

## What makes a letter work

**Show, do not tell.** This is the whole game.

> Not: *She meets deadlines.*
> But: *I sent her requests for edits at 10 a.m. and she returned the revision by
> 3 p.m. the same day.*

> Not: *He is a good citizen.*
> But: *Before he was a TA, he spent several hours a week helping one of our
> students who was struggling with the fall quarter course in statistics.*

> *Her story stood out. It was funny, witty and memorable. It's the only one I
> remember vividly from the class.*

Going back through your email to find the exact timing of that turnaround is
worth the five minutes.

**Quantify and compare.** All letters are inflated, so specific calibration is
what a committee can actually use.

> *She is among the top 10 % of Master's students I have taught at Stanford.*

Top 1 %, 5 %, 10 % — and out of what group.

**Length is signal.** For a candidate you rate highly, go onto a second page.
Committees read several short one-page letters as collective lack of enthusiasm.

**Bring in other voices.** Other faculty's opinions, a co-instructor's paragraph,
a quotation from an unsolicited student email. External corroboration is more
credible than your own praise repeated.

**Mention extenuating circumstances** where relevant. A difficult background or
major family upheaval matters, especially against a weaker academic record.
Reviewers value candidates who have overcome unusual obstacles.

Emphasis with bold or italics is fine and does get noticed.

**Write it well.** A beautifully written letter makes a reader want to interview
the candidate on the strength of the letter alone.

---

## What weakens a letter

**Talking about yourself.** Letters that dwell on the writer's course, the
writer's project, the writer's field are filling space — and a reader recognises
that as *I have little to say about this person*. Unless that is your intention,
keep the letter about them.

**Reciting the CV.** They have the CV. Adding nothing to it adds nothing to the
application.

One writer handled an exceptional candidate by listing her accolades at the very
end, then adding:

> *These are items I would have highlighted at the beginning of any other
> student's recommendation letter. The fact that I nearly forgot to include this
> list speaks volumes.*

---

## The calibrated language

Letters have a hidden register. Every statement below is positive; each says
something quite different to an experienced reader.

| What you write | What a committee hears |
|---|---|
| *Though not the top student in the class, he held his own among an extremely gifted group.* | good, not outstanding |
| *He was one of the best students in my class of 50.* | solid; not the best I have seen |
| *He was the best student in my class of 50.* | outstanding; not the best of my career |
| *She is one of the most brilliant and accomplished students I have taught to date.* | do not pass this person up |

Reserve the top tier for candidates who genuinely warrant it — five letters in
fifteen years, in the course's account. Your credibility and judgement are what
give that sentence its force, and spending it cheaply destroys it.

The same calibration operates in smaller phrasings:

- *I have confidence in her ability* vs *I have no doubt that she will go on to do
  first-rate research.* The second is much stronger, and choosing the first tells
  the reader something.
- *She is the most enthusiastic student I have ever worked with* sounds warm until
  you ask why not *most talented*, *most accomplished*, *most brilliant*. When the
  highest praise available is *enthusiastic*, *hardworking* or *a team player*, a
  reader infers the person is not a top performer.

---

## If you are the one asking

- **Respect their time.** Several weeks' notice, never one.
- **Choose carefully.** Pick people who know you well and think well of you. One
  clearly negative letter sinks an otherwise strong application.
- **A decline is information, not an obstacle.** Do not press. It usually means
  they do not feel able to write you a strong letter, which may simply mean they
  do not know you well enough.
- **If they ask you to draft it yourself, go elsewhere.** It signals they will not
  invest the time.
- **Make it easy.** CV, the specific opportunity, deadline and link in one place.
  Offer to meet.
- **Plan ahead.** If you are enjoying a course and doing well in it, go to office
  hours, ask for advice, make a connection. Being remembered later is not
  manipulation; it is how anyone gets a letter with a story in it.

# Part 2 — Personal statements

Used for graduate and medical admissions, fellowships, scholarships, internships
and some jobs.

## The core requirement

**It should be personal, and it should feel a little risky to write.** A stilted
list of accomplishments with no personality earns the word *blah* in a reviewer's
notes, and blah is the most common outcome.

One memorable statement described growing up between two opposing cultural
worlds, then connected that to balancing opposing forces in medicine — wanting to
spend real time with patients against having to be efficient. It revealed
something true and then made it relevant.

## The rules

**Show, do not tell.** Do not claim commitment to global health; show it. Readers
do not remember abstractions.

**Do not recite the CV.** They have it. Highlight one publication and tell them
what the work was actually like — what was hard, what you figured out, how it
felt. The competence, persistence and problem-solving come through without being
claimed.

> Weak: *I have submitted or published three first-author publications and I am a
> co-author on nine additional publications.*
>
> Better: *Dr Smith and I are currently drafting a manuscript reporting our
> findings for journal submission. My work at IU has been invaluable, as it
> helped me gain insight into the challenges of recruiting and retaining
> vulnerable patients in a study.*

**Do not use words you are not sure of.** *I want to better understand
epidemiology and biostatistics doctrines* — *doctrine* means a body of belief
laid down as incontrovertible, which is not what scientific principles are. And
no clichés.

**Flatter the programme, specifically.** You are writing to people proud of what
they built. Show you know it, show how you fit its mission, name professors whose
work you know. This demonstrates you did not send the same essay to twelve
places.

**Address weaknesses head on.** A poor transcript, low scores, a gap — reviewers
will notice regardless. Silence gets the application discarded; a candid account
showing self-awareness and growth gets it noticed. This is exactly the
limitations section of a manuscript: anticipate the criticism and beat the reader
to it.

**Never embellish or invent.**

## Structure

**Opening** — strong, compelling, memorable. A story or a scene beats a thesis
statement. A longer first paragraph is fine if it earns the space.

> *Growing up in Tanzania, I heard a lot about HIV/AIDS and its effects on our
> population. But it was not until my cousin contracted the virus and
> subsequently passed it on to her newborn baby that it really hit home. At age
> ten, I was shattered by the terrible news, and promised myself that one day I
> would do something to tackle the epidemic and help those affected.*

Compare the version to avoid, which is common:

> *Inspired by the courses I have taken at X College, I have decided that I want
> to pursue a doctoral degree in Y. As a double major of A and B at X College, I
> gained excellent skills in C, D and E. Thus, I am well-prepared for doctoral
> studies.*

Safe, boring, and entirely available from the CV.

**Body**, roughly one to two paragraphs each:

1. Where do you want to go — short- and long-term ambitions.
2. What experiences led you here — personal history that shows the motivation.
3. What makes you a strong candidate — specifics, illustrated with stories; and
   this is where weaknesses get turned into obstacles overcome.
4. Why this programme, specifically.

**Conclusion** — summarise your interest and **circle back to the opening**. If
you started with a patient, say what happened to that patient. If you started
with an image, return to it. Being lofty and aspirational is allowed here.

> *The opportunity to work with your team would put me smack in the middle of one
> of the most exciting research labs on the planet. It would give me a wealth of
> experience that would not only inspire beautiful writing, but would forever be
> woven into my future tales.*

**Tailor to the opportunity.** For a science-writing internship on a research ship
in Antarctica, where motion sickness was a genuine selection concern, one writer
described biking from Paris to Barcelona and years of competitive distance
running, closing with *incidentally, I travelled so much for competitions in
college that I developed an excellent knack for writing on buses, planes and
trains. I expect this would extend easily to ships.* Adaptability, endurance,
focus and dedication all conveyed, none of them named.
