# The research plan

Source: module 7.4 (Sky Brubaker, Stanford).

The specific aims page names a need and promises a payoff. **The research plan is
the bridge between them.** Its job is to convince a reviewer that you can get from
the one to the other, in the time available, with the resources you have.

The bridge is also the course's teaching analogy, and it works well enough to keep:
imagine proposing to build a bridge from San Francisco to Marin. Is there a need?
If so, why has nobody built it? What barrier is in the way? How exactly will you
build it, what might go wrong, how long will it take, and what becomes possible
afterwards?

---

## Outline

```
1. Background and significance
2. Aims                          ← the bulk of the document
   for each aim:
     introduction
     preliminary data
     methods
     expected outcomes
     alternative approaches
3. Timeline
4. Conclusions and future directions
```

---

## 1. Background and significance

Establish the importance of the problem:

- the premise for the proposed work
- strengths and weaknesses of the published research
- your preliminary data

These lead to the **knowledge gap or technical deficiency** the project will
overcome. It must be the same gap named in the specific aims — the two documents
tell one story.

**Innovation.** Many applications require an explicit statement. Innovation is
usually about improved approaches or methods rather than a wholly new idea: what
does your proposal do better than previous work? New methods, new technology,
better experimental design.

---

## 2. The aims

The bulk of the plan. Break each aim into five parts.

### Introduction — about one paragraph

A conceptual overview of why *this aim* matters: the specific objective, the
working hypothesis, the rationale behind it, and the expected outcomes.

### Preliminary data

Length depends on space and on how much you have. Include a critical review of
the relevant literature and the studies that establish feasibility.

**The data here must stand alone**, outside the framework of the grant. Figure
legends should be fuller than in a paper — background, and interpretation of what
the figure shows. You are walking the reviewer through your evidence so that they
reach the conclusion themselves: this person and this project can succeed.

### Methods

A detailed description of the experimental design for the aim:

- validation of essential reagents and approaches
- controls, and why they are the right controls
- statistical analysis
- what interpretations the resulting data will support

**Use confident verbs.** *We expect*, *this will*, *the assay can* — not *we
hope*, *we will try*. Hedging in a methods section reads as doubt about your own
design.

### Expected outcomes

Summarise what you expect each experiment to produce, and interpret it. What is
the immediate payoff? Does it address the gap you set out to bridge?

### Alternative approaches

Introduce them by naming the potential problem: what if the expected outcome does
not materialise?

This section is not an admission of weakness. It is evidence that you have
thought the project through, and a reviewer who cannot see a fallback will invent
the objection themselves.

*In the bridge analogy: if the new material's strength is in question, it would
be used only for the less critical portions of the structure.*

---

## 3. Timeline

Demonstrates feasibility, which is what bridges the need and the payoff in the
reviewer's mind. Year 1: support structure complete. Year 2: surface structure
complete. Support the schedule with whatever evidence you have.

**Show it visually.** A Gantt-style chart lets a reviewer absorb overlapping
projects at a glance, and it saves space — which in a page-limited application is
not a small consideration.

---

## 4. Conclusions and future directions

Summarise the expected outcomes and how they bridge the knowledge gap and advance
the field. Then look beyond: what experiments or approaches become possible once
this is done?

Both the immediate payoff and the longer-term one belong here. *We can now
explore Marin — and with the new bridge technology, we may one day build one to
Hawaii.*

---

## The test

Read the finished plan and ask whether all four are unmistakable:

- [ ] There is a **need**.
- [ ] There is a **payoff**, immediate and future.
- [ ] It is clear **how** the project gets from one to the other.
- [ ] It is clear **how long** that takes, and why that is realistic.

A good research plan always bridges the gap between the need and the expected
payoff. If a reviewer can see the need and the payoff but not the span between
them, the proposal fails on feasibility, which is the most common way strong
ideas lose.
