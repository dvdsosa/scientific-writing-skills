# The specific aims page

Source: module 7.3 (Crystal Botham, Stanford Biosciences Grant Writing Academy).

One page. It is the executive summary of the whole proposal, and it does three
jobs at once:

1. **It is your roadmap.** Write it first and the rest of the proposal has a
   spine.
2. **It is what people will actually read when you ask for feedback.** Colleagues
   and mentors will give real comments on one page; few will read forty.
3. **It must be able to win the grant on its own.** Assume a reviewer who reads
   only this page still has everything needed to argue for funding you.

---

## The instruction, and what it is really asking

NIH's wording:

> State concisely the goals of the proposed research and summarize expected
> outcomes, including the impact the results of the proposed research will exert
> on the research fields involved. List succinctly the specific objectives of the
> research proposed, e.g., to test a stated hypothesis, create a novel design,
> solve a specific problem, challenge an existing paradigm or clinical practice,
> address a critical barrier to progress in the field, or develop new technology.

The keywords are **goals, expected outcomes, impact, specific objectives**, and
they resolve into four questions.

---

## The four questions

Everything on the page answers one of these, and — this is the part people miss —
**the four answers must be aligned with each other**, like gears. If the overall
goal does not fill the gap you named, or the expected payoff does not follow from
the aims, the page does not work no matter how well written each part is.

### 1. Is the research question important?

There is a gap in the knowledge base, or an unmet need, that is holding the field
back. Fill it and the field moves more than incrementally.

### 2. What is the overall goal?

It must address the gap named in question 1. And it **must be attainable
regardless of how the hypothesis tests** — if your goal only exists in the world
where your hypothesis is right, you are asking a funder to bet on a result rather
than on a programme of work.

### 3. What specifically will be done?

The aims themselves, the working hypothesis, and the approach that objectively
tests it.

### 4. What are the expected outcomes and impact?

The return on investment. What can reviewers expect to be true about the field
once this is finished?

---

## Turning the four questions into four sections

### Section 1 — importance

**First sentence must grab the reviewer**, and should signal that the proposal
supports the funder's mission.

Then bring them up to speed: relevant current knowledge, enough that a reviewer
outside your specific subfield understands what follows. They may not know your
field's complexities.

**End with an explicit knowledge-gap or need statement.** This is the sentence
that drives the entire proposal. One faculty example ends the paragraph:

> *Thus, there is a critical need to standardize how donor hearts are evaluated
> and accepted for transplantation.*

By the end of this section the reviewer should be interested, should see the area
as relevant, and should accept the gap as important.

### Section 2 — from the gap down to your project

Funnel: start broad, narrow to specifics.

- **Long-term goal** — for your research programme, or tied directly to the
  funder's mission.
- **Objective of this proposal** — narrower, and it must address the gap
  statement above. Attainable either way the hypothesis tests.
- **Central hypothesis** — most funders fund hypothesis-driven work, so state it
  explicitly. It must be objectively testable, and it is your prediction, your
  best bet. **Bold or italicise it**: it is the climax of this section.
- **Preliminary data**, or whatever data led you to formulate the hypothesis.

### Section 3 — the aims

Broad aims that remain achievable regardless of how the hypotheses test. Use
**attention-getting headline text** for each aim — reviewers skim, and the aim
headlines are what they skim. Then, briefly, the methods that will test each
hypothesis, and the working hypotheses under each aim.

### Section 4 — expected payoff

The return on investment, tied to the funder's mission. For a fellowship or career
development award, where the funder's goal is to advance you toward independence,
your own development belongs here.

Ending on the payoff is deliberate: it leaves the reviewer holding the positive
impact as they turn the page.

---

## The order is not fixed

Two funded examples in the course use different arrangements. One follows the
sequence above exactly. An NIH F31 graduate fellowship puts *is the question
important* in the first half of paragraph one, the gap statement in the middle,
and the overall goal in the second half of the same paragraph, with the hypothesis
broken out separately afterwards.

What both do: answer all four questions, keep them aligned, make the hypothesis
easy to find, use bold headline aims, and end on the payoff.

---

## Feedback is the method, not a final step

Get feedback at the beginning, the middle and the end. Writing the specific aims
is iterative by nature. Because your reviewers will come from a wide range of
backgrounds, your readers should too — a non-specialist who cannot follow your
gap statement has told you something important, not revealed their own ignorance.

---

## Checklist

- [ ] Fits one page, within the format rules
- [ ] First sentence grabs, and connects to the funder's mission
- [ ] Enough background for a reviewer outside the subfield
- [ ] An explicit knowledge-gap or need statement
- [ ] Long-term goal, then objective, then hypothesis, narrowing
- [ ] Hypothesis is objectively testable, and visually marked
- [ ] Objective attainable regardless of how the hypothesis tests
- [ ] Aims have headline text a skimmer can read
- [ ] Expected payoff stated, tied to the funder's mission
- [ ] All four answers are consistent with one another
- [ ] Read by at least one person outside your field
