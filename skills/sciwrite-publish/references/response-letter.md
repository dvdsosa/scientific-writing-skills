# Responding to reviewers

Source: module 6.3.

---

## Before you write anything

Read the reviews, then put them down for a day.

Your first reaction to criticism will be defensive. Everyone's is. The skill —
and it is a general life skill, not a writing one — is to shorten the time
between reading the criticism and being able to use it. The faster you stop
taking it as a judgement of you and start treating it as information about the
paper, the easier this gets.

By the time you write the letter, you will usually find the reviews improved the
paper. Say so, and mean it.

---

## Structure

```
Dear Dr <Editor>,

We thank you and the reviewers for the careful reading of our manuscript and
for comments that have substantially improved it. We have addressed each point
below and have marked all changes in the tracked-changes version.

REVIEWER 1

1. <Reviewer's comment, quoted or closely paraphrased.>

   We agree. We have <what you did>, and the revised text now reads
   <quote or short summary>. See page 7, paragraph 2.

2. <Comment.>

   <Response.>

REVIEWER 2

1. ...

Yours sincerely,
<corresponding author, on behalf of all authors>
```

Mirror their numbering exactly. An editor comparing your letter to the reviews
should never have to search.

---

## Phrasing that works

**Agreeing and changing:**

> We agree with Reviewers 1 and 2 that the section on X was too abbreviated. We
> have therefore added a paragraph that highlights <…>, now on page 5, lines
> 12–24.

Always name the location. Reviewers verify.

**Agreeing partially:**

> We take the reviewer's point that Y may confound the result. We have added an
> analysis excluding those participants (new Table S2); the direction and
> magnitude of the effect are unchanged. We have added two sentences to the
> Limitations paragraph noting this.

An analysis is a stronger answer than a sentence, when one is available.

**Disagreeing:**

> We appreciate this suggestion but have chosen not to make this change, for the
> following reason. <Reason, one or two sentences, technical and specific.> We
> have, however, made the underlying assumption explicit on page 9 so that
> readers can judge it for themselves.

Give a thoughtful, respectful, specific reason — and where possible, concede
something adjacent. Refusing outright with no accommodation is what makes an
editor side with the reviewer.

**When the reviewer has misunderstood:**

Assume first that the misunderstanding is your fault, because it usually is.

> The reviewer understood our procedure as <X>. In fact we did <Y>. We have
> rewritten the paragraph on page 4 to make this unambiguous, since the original
> wording clearly invited the other reading.

This is stronger than "the reviewer is mistaken", and it is usually more accurate.

**Small requests you disagree with:** just make the change. Spending capital on
a trivial point costs more than the point is worth. Save disagreement for things
that matter.

## When the reviewer is wrong but something is still broken

Worth checking every time, because it is remarkably common: a reviewer who
identifies the wrong problem has usually noticed a real one.

The course's case: a reviewer discussed the background literature incorrectly.
The authors' first instinct was that the reviewer did not know the field. Looking
closer, they had numbered their own references wrong — the reviewer was reading
the citation as given and attributing a finding to the wrong paper. The reviewer
was wrong about the literature and right that something was broken.

So when a comment seems off-target, ask: *what in the manuscript could have
produced this reading?* If the answer is "nothing", say so politely. If the
answer is "our wording", fix the wording.

---

## Also include

- **A tracked-changes version.** Most journals require it, and reviewers use it
  to check the changes were made.
- **A clean version** for the record.
- Any new supplementary material you generated in response.

---

## Things that go wrong

- **Answering in prose rather than point by point.** The editor cannot map your
  paragraphs onto the reviewers' concerns, and will assume you skipped some.
- **Claiming a change you did not make.** Reviewers verify against the tracked
  changes.
- **Not saying where the change is.** "We have clarified this" without a location
  makes the reviewer hunt.
- **Sarcasm, or wounded dignity.** Both are visible in writing and neither helps.
- **Silence on a comment you found annoying.** An unanswered point reads as
  evasion. Answer everything, even if the answer is a considered "no".
