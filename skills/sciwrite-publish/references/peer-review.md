# Doing a peer review

Source: module 6.7.

---

## Why accept the invitation

Editors want junior reviewers. Two reasons, both practical: younger reviewers are
closer to current methods, and they are more careful. Review quality
demonstrably deteriorates with experience — the more you do, the faster you get,
and the less careful you are.

What you get from it: a view of the back end of publication; the chance to read
the other reviewers' comments on the same paper, which is both instructive and
reassuring; and the realisation, from seeing what gets submitted, that your own
work is more competitive than you thought.

Your first review will take a very long time — a day is normal — because you feel
the responsibility. That is exactly why editors want you. It gets faster.

---

## The order to read in

1. **Skim the abstract**, just to know the topic.
2. **Go straight to the tables and figures.** Look at the data before reading the
   authors' interpretation of it, so your judgement is your own. This matters:
   authors sometimes see in their data what they hoped to find.
3. **Read the paper through once, quickly**, for high-level impressions:
   - Do the conclusions match the data? This is where papers most often fail.
   - Is the writing comprehensible at all?
   - Is the paper too long for the amount of new information in it?
4. **Then go section by section**, taking notes as you go.

---

## What to look for, section by section

**Introduction** — clarity, concision, and a clear statement of the hypothesis or
aims. If you cannot find what the study set out to do, that is a finding.

**Methods** — how were the data collected? Where could bias have entered? Was
there a proper control? Were the right statistics used? Could you replicate this?

**Results** — clarity and concision; does the text *complement* the tables and
figures, or just recite them?

**Tables and figures** — does each stand alone? Does each tell a clear story? Are
any misleading (a fitted line implying a relationship the data do not support, a
truncated axis)? Do the numbers agree across tables, figures and text?

**Discussion** — does the first paragraph say plainly what was found? Are the
conclusions justified, or overreaching? Does the limitations section address the
things that actually threaten this study's validity, or generic boilerplate?

---

## Tone

You are hunting for problems, so a critical, condescending register creeps in
without your noticing. Being critical does not require sounding critical.

> **Harsh:** The authors should delete Table 5. Not only is it completely
> irrelevant, but it also reveals their utter lack of statistical understanding.
>
> **Same criticism, usable:** Table 5 contains unnecessary information; for
> example, a Pearson's correlation coefficient may not be appropriate here. The
> authors should consider revising or omitting the table.

Note what changed: the criticism is aimed at the **table**, not the authors; it
gives a specific reason; and it offers a route forward.

Practical rules:

- **Critique the work, not the authors.** Draft naturally, then go back and
  convert "the authors got this wrong" into "this analysis has a problem".
- **Avoid generalisations.** "The paper is poorly written" is unusable. Name
  sentences.
- **Prefer positive framing.** "The writing and presentation could be improved,
  for example…" over "the paper is poorly written".
- **Do not lecture.** The urge to teach the authors statistics or writing is
  strong and reads as condescension. Not your job here.

Remember who is on the other end: usually a graduate student who did the work and
whose confidence is at stake.

If it helps, note that reviewers write more constructively when they know the
review will be published. That instinct is available to you whether or not the
journal is open.

---

## Structure of the review

**1. One paragraph of general overview, before any specifics.**

- State what you take to be the major finding and importance of the work. This
  starts on a positive note, and it tells the authors what actually came across —
  useful information if it is not what they intended.
- Then genuine positive statements. There are always some: if the methods are
  weak, perhaps the writing is strong; perhaps the question itself is novel.
  Starting with the positive makes everything after it land better.
- Then the one or two major, big-picture limitations, if you are not recommending
  acceptance as is. Missing control group, overstated findings, presentation too
  confusing to evaluate.

**Do not reveal your recommendation.** Accept/reject goes to the editor, not into
the comments to authors.

**2. A numbered list of specific criticisms.**

Typically five to fifteen. Fewer if you are recommending outright acceptance
(little to say) or outright rejection (focus only on the fatal flaws). More for
revise-and-resubmit, because there you are telling the authors exactly what to
fix.

Be specific. A generalisation the author cannot act on is wasted words.

**3. Optional confidential comments to the editor.**

Where you can be franker, and where ethical concerns — suspected plagiarism,
undisclosed conflicts — belong.

**4. The grading sheet**, if the journal uses one: impact, interest to the
journal's readership, originality, overall rank. These feel arbitrary; just be
internally consistent.

---

## You are not a copy editor

Do not list every typo and grammatical slip. Journals employ copy editors. If the
language genuinely needs work, say so once in general terms, give one or two
examples, and recommend the authors seek copy editing or English-language help.
Reviewers who spend their effort on commas are not doing the job the editor
needs done.

---

## Types of review you may encounter

**Single-blind** — authors do not know the reviewers; reviewers know the authors.
The most common.

**Double-blind** — both are blinded. Imperfect (a determined reviewer can usually
work out who wrote a paper in a narrow field) but it does reduce bias.

**Open** — nobody is blinded, and the reviewer's name and full text may be
published. This tends to make reviews more constructive.

**Post-publication** — blogs, social media, and formal channels. An increasingly
important layer for papers that got through review with problems intact.
