# Plagiarism and self-plagiarism

Source: module 6.1.

Everyone knows not to steal a paragraph. The line sits further out than most
people assume, and the cases that end careers are usually not the blatant ones.

---

## What counts

All of the following are plagiarism:

- Copying a sentence, or **part of a sentence**, into your work as if you wrote it.
- Copying someone's text and then rearranging the words or swapping in synonyms.
  Those changes are cosmetic; the words and the thought are still theirs.
- Borrowing a description or definition from Wikipedia.
- Copying a **summary of the literature** — including its citations — from another
  paper.
- Mimicking the original author's sentence structure closely while changing the
  vocabulary.

### The synonym-substitution case

Wikipedia:

> *Ernest Miller Hemingway was an American author and journalist. His economical
> and understated style had a strong influence on 20th century fiction, while his
> life of adventure and his public image influenced later generations.*

A student's version:

> *Ernest Hemingway's thrifty and understated style strongly influenced 20th
> century fiction. His audacious lifestyle and public image also influenced later
> generations.*

*Economical* became *thrifty*; *life of adventure* became *audacious lifestyle*;
*had a strong influence* became *strongly influenced*. This is plagiarism, and
the reason is diagnostic:

> I could have rewritten that text without having any understanding of Hemingway
> or of what a thrifty or understated style even means. — module 6.1

That is the test. If you could produce your version without understanding the
subject, you have not written anything.

### The subtle case, which is probably the common one

A 2009 paper contained:

> *Recent registry-based and hospital-based studies have documented a
> statistically significant increased risk of melanoma after breast cancer with
> standardized incident ratios ranging from 1.4 to 2.7. [1,2,3,4]*

Its own reference 5, a 2004 paper, contained the identical sentence — with the
same four citations. There was no other sign of plagiarism anywhere in the 2009
paper.

Two things are wrong with this, and the second is the one people miss:

1. The 2004 authors did the work — searching the literature, synthesising it,
   putting it into words — and the 2009 authors took it.
2. The 2009 authors are **vouching for numbers they never checked**, and assuming
   nothing relevant appeared between 2004 and 2009.

Quotation marks and attribution would have fixed the first problem and, usefully,
the second: quoting says *these are the numbers according to someone else*, not
*I have verified these*. But the right answer was to search the literature
themselves.

## How to avoid it, procedurally

The defence is a working habit, not vigilance:

1. **Read multiple sources**, not one. You cannot write originally about a topic
   you know only through a single account.
2. **When collecting material, paste quotations inside quotation marks**, in your
   notes, as you paste them. Months later you will not remember which words were
   yours.
3. **Write from memory and understanding**, never with someone else's text open
   in front of you. If you find yourself moving their words around, stop: you are
   not ready to write that passage yet.
4. **Do not trust that the source got it right.** Go to primary sources and check
   the numbers yourself.

## Self-plagiarism

Recycling your own published text or data into a new paper. Also not allowed.

- If you are rehashing old material, why is there a new paper? Having nothing new
  to say is the underlying problem.
- The journal that published the original usually owns the copyright.

**The one accepted exception:** some duplication in Materials and Methods. There
are only so many ways to describe the same cell preparation, and if you used the
same protocol across several studies, editors are generally fine with repeated
phrasing there. It should not be a wholesale copy, and it does not extend to the
introduction, results or discussion.

**Data duplication** is the more serious version: republishing a dataset with
minor additions or tweaks as if it were new. This misleads readers into treating
two papers as two independent pieces of evidence for the same hypothesis, and it
pads the literature.

---

## How common

- Pilot tests of CrossCheck found journals rejecting **6–23 %** of submitted
  manuscripts outright for plagiarism (including self-plagiarism).
- A study using automated detection with manual confirmation found **8 %** of
  papers plagiarised others' work and about **3 %** involved self-plagiarism.
- Among roughly 5,000 residency personal statements at one prestigious hospital,
  **5 %** showed clear evidence of plagiarism — one in twenty applying doctors.

---

## Detecting it

Take a full sentence from the suspect text, put it in quotation marks, and search
for it. The probability that two people independently produce the same string of
seven to ten words is negligible. Common phrases and clichés run to four or five
words; by ten, coincidence is not a plausible explanation.

If one sentence hits, check others. Plagiarism is rarely isolated — one course
case, a commissioned review article, turned out to have taken one sentence from
one paper, two from another, and several from a third.

The most extreme case in the course: a 2002 randomised trial whose discussion
reproduced a 1995 trial's discussion nearly verbatim, including its numbered list
of explanations for a negative result, with a handful of words changed (*dose of
estrogen* → *estrogen dose*, *bone mass can* → *bone mass may*). One of the four
explanations was dropped, apparently to cover the tracks. Almost the entire text
of the later paper came from the earlier one; only the data differed. The authors
were writing in a second language and appear to have used the earlier paper as a
template — which explains the behaviour without excusing it, and points at the
right solution: get language help, not a template.
