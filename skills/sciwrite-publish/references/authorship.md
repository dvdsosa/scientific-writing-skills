# Authorship

Source: module 6.2.

Decide authorship **before the paper is written**, especially if you are the one
drafting it. Establish who is on it and in what order, run that past everyone
early, and you avoid the conversation where someone feels slighted after the
fact.

---

## Who qualifies

One criterion governs: an author **takes public responsibility for the content**.

That is a high bar, and it cuts both ways. If the paper is later found to contain
errors or fraud, or is retracted, every author owns that. So before accepting
co-authorship on someone else's paper, ask yourself whether you understand its
ins and outs well enough to defend it.

Junior researchers systematically over-include, feeling an obligation to add
every senior person they ever discussed the work with. This is backwards. Most
people **do not want** to be on a paper they were not substantially involved in,
precisely because of the responsibility, and they will not be offended by the
omission. Someone who gave you statistical advice or edited a draft belongs in
the acknowledgements.

---

## Order

Order signals relative contribution.

- **First author** — usually wrote the draft, and often collected the data. Often
  a graduate student or junior researcher.
- **Last author** — usually the senior investigator who oversaw the work. Also a
  position of stature.
- **In between** — by contribution. If contributions are genuinely
  indistinguishable, alphabetical or reverse-alphabetical order is an acceptable
  convention; say which you used.
- **Dual first authorship** — when two people contributed equally. Standard, and
  worth using rather than fighting over the slot.
- **Large collaborations** — hundreds of authors can be cited as a named group.

Conventions differ by field. Check what yours does before assuming.

---

## Acknowledgements

- **Funding sources.** Always. Fellowships, grants, institutional support.
- **People who helped but do not meet the authorship bar** — supplied materials,
  gave statistical consultation, offered advice, read a draft.

This section is the pressure valve that lets you keep the author list honest.

---

## Ghost authorship

A professional writer — typically hired by a company — drafts the manuscript and
does not appear as an author.

There is nothing wrong with professional writers helping scientists write. The
problem is the concealment: the writer is likely a paid advocate for the sponsor,
and nothing in the paper says so.

---

## Guest (honorary) authorship

A company runs the study, analyses the data, draws the conclusions, drafts the
paper — and then invites an academic at a prestigious institution to be an author,
often first author.

That academic cannot take public responsibility for the content; they were not
involved in the analysis or the writing. What they supply is credibility, and
what they receive is a high-profile publication on their CV. The reader is left
believing an independent expert drove the work.

**How common:** a 2011 survey of top medical journals found evidence of honorary
or guest authorship in 17.6 % of papers, and ghost authorship in 7.6 %. Journals
have since tightened their rules, so these numbers are probably lower now.

**How it looks from the inside.** Litigation over the drug Vioxx put Merck's
internal documents in the public domain, and a 2008 *JAMA* analysis of 250 court
documents found employees preparing manuscripts and then recruiting external
academic authors, who were frequently placed first or second in the author list.
For review articles, marketing staff planned the manuscripts, contracted medical
publishing companies to ghostwrite them, and recruited academics — often as sole
author, sometimes for an honorarium. Only half of those reviews disclosed the
sponsorship.

An actual email from that record:

> I would like to invite you to be an author on the abstract and manuscript for
> this study… In making your decision, you may want to take into consideration
> that the results of this study were negative at first glance.

Authorship offered as a gift, declinable depending on how the results came out.
Whatever else that is, it is not taking responsibility for content.

---

## The practical test

Before you put your name on something, ask: *if a journalist called me tomorrow
and asked me to defend the methods and the conclusions of this paper, could I?*

If not, ask for the acknowledgements instead.
