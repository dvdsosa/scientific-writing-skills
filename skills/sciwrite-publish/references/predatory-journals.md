# Predatory journals

Source: module 6.8.

Open-access publishing, done with integrity, is a good thing: authors pay a
publication fee so that the full text is free to everyone. PLOS is the standard
example of it working.

But money changing hands opened a door. **Predatory open-access journals** exist
to collect author fees, not to publish research. They perform little or no peer
review, and they will accept nearly anything.

---

## The evidence that they accept anything

John Bohannon's 2013 sting, published in *Science*: he generated 255 variants of
a single obviously flawed cancer paper — a scientific Mad Libs on the template
*molecule X from lichen species Y inhibits the growth of cancer cell Z* — with
defects any scientist should catch in Figure 1.

Of the 255 journals that returned a decision, **157 accepted the paper**. Only 98
rejected it. Among publishers on Jeffrey Beall's list of suspect operations,
82 % accepted it. (PLOS ONE correctly rejected it.)

**Scale and growth.** A 2015 *BMC Medicine* study tracked these journals from
2010 to 2014: roughly **8,000 active predatory journals** by 2014, publishing
about **420,000 articles** that year, up from ~53,000 in 2010. Average article
processing charge: about **US$178**. No discipline is unaffected.

## The checklist

Declan Butler's, published in *Nature* in 2013:

- [ ] The publisher provides **full, verifiable contact information**, including a
      real postal address.
- [ ] The **editorial board lists real experts with full affiliations**. Contact
      one and ask about their experience with the publisher — names have been
      taken and posted without permission.
- [ ] The journal **displays its author-fee policy prominently**. Open-access
      journals do charge; a legitimate one is not coy about how much.
- [ ] You did not arrive via a **spam invitation**. Real journals rarely solicit.
- [ ] The **articles they have published are of decent quality**. Go and read
      some. This alone settles most cases.
- [ ] The **peer-review process is clearly described** — and appears to happen.
- [ ] The **claimed impact factor is real.**

## Fake metrics

A growth industry. Names engineered to sound like the real thing:

- Universal Impact Factor (UIF)
- Global Impact Factor (GIF)
- Journal Impact Factor (JIF)

The genuine article is the Journal Impact Factor from Clarivate's *Journal
Citation Reports*. If a journal advertises an impact factor, verify it at the
source rather than believing the number on the site.

## Whitelists

Imperfect, but the journals listed have been through some vetting:

- **DOAJ** — Directory of Open Access Journals
- **OASPA** — Open Access Scholarly Publishers Association

Check before submitting.

## Recognising academic spam

You will get several of these a day. The tells:

- Wild flattery and exuberance (*we are enthralled by the prospect…*)
- Multiple exclamation marks
- Grammatical errors throughout
- A topic unrelated to anything you work on
- Urgency and flattery combined (*this 2017 may you output the best papers of
  your career and get the best number of citations*)
- An invitation to join an editorial board of a journal you have never heard of,
  addressed to someone at your career stage

Someone actually studied this systematically (*BMJ* Christmas issue, 2016): five
academics received an average of **312 spam invitations each month**;
unsubscribing cut the rate by 39 % after a month but only 19 % after a year;
16 % of invitations were duplicates and **83 % had little or no relevance** to
the recipient.

---

## Use common sense

If it looks like spam, it is spam. If a journal promises review in seventy-two
hours, that is not a service, it is an admission.
